How I traded Python scripts for muddy boots to tackle reed bed restoration in Northern Italy's Lake Orta

This summer I stepped away from my usual data science work to help restore vanishing reed beds (Phragmites australis) around Lake Orta in Northern Italy. The challenge: understand why these crucial ecosystems disappeared and design a hands-on restoration experiment. I identified three main pressure sources (water level fluctuations, animal predation, nutrient limitations) and implemented a small-scale field trial to test solutions. Early results show promise.

Introduction - Why Reed Beds Matter (and Why They're Disappearing)

Reed beds might look like just another patch of tall grass by the water, but they're actually ecosystem powerhouses. These dense stands of Phragmites australis (the common reed) provide crucial nesting habitat for waterbirds, protect shorelines from erosion, and act as natural water filters. Think of them as nature's multitasking champions.

Quick explainer on Phragmites australis

Phragmites australis is particularly important across European freshwater ecosystems. This hardy perennial can grow up to 3.5 meters tall and forms dense colonies through underground rhizomes, creating stable habitats that can persist for decades. In healthy conditions, these reed beds intercept up to 99% of incoming light, creating unique microenvironments that support specialized flora and fauna. They're essentially the apartment buildings of wetland ecosystems, providing structure and shelter for countless species.

At Lake Orta in northern Italy, these reed beds had been a familiar sight for decades, particularly around the southern bays of the lake. Then, starting around 2010, they began disappearing. By 2023, what had once been thriving vegetation around the lake's bays was completely gone. The ecological impact was immediate: fewer nesting birds, a lake ecosystem out of balance and an increased risk of shore erosion.

When I was asked to help understand and address this decline, it felt like a perfect match for my background in environmental monitoring and data analysis. But this project would push me in a different direction — away from satellite imagery and Python scripts, toward boots-in-the-mud field work.

Detective Work - Understanding the Decline

Before jumping into any restoration work, I needed to understand what killed the reeds in the first place. Fortunately, Dr. Roberto Dellavedova (EURAC - Institute for Alpine Environment) had conducted a comprehensive census of Lake Orta's reed beds in 2021-2022, producing the FloraLago report. This document became my starting point for a forensic analysis of an ecosystem in decline.

The report revealed that the collapse wasn't sudden. Reed beds had been shrinking since at least 2013, with the most dramatic losses concentrated around Gozzano Bay in the southern part of the lake. By 2025, what had once been thriving reed communities were completely gone.

Digging into the data, three main pressure sources emerged:

Water level fluctuations proved to be a major culprit. Climate change has brought more extreme weather patterns to northern Italy, causing dramatic lake level swings. Phragmites needs consistent moisture conditions — too dry and the plants stress, too deep and young shoots can't establish.

Animal predation presented another challenge. The lake's ecosystem had changed over the decades, with new arrivals like musket-rats (large South American rodents) joining native swans and various insects in feeding on reed shoots and rhizomes. Swans and ducks also tend to eat the young shoots.

Substrate and nutrient limitations created a third challenge. Phragmites australis is essentially a nitrogen junkie: it requires extremely high nitrogen levels to maintain its rapid growth and dense colony formation. Lake sediments can become depleted over time, especially in areas where nutrient inputs have changed. Additionally, reeds need a stable substrate where their rhizomes can properly anchor and spread.

Management and wave pressure added another layer of complexity. Traditional reed management involved annual cutting, which removed dead material and promoted healthy regrowth. Without this maintenance, aging reed colonies became less vigorous, with accumulated dead material creating conditions that favored fungal infections over healthy growth. Wave action from increased boat traffic also could play a role, breaking young shoots before they could establish and eroding the shoreline substrate that reeds depend on.

What made the situation worse was that previous restoration efforts had failed spectacularly. In 2023, the provincial government attempted to restore the reeds using wooden wave barriers and transplanted shoots from Lake Garda. Within a year, the barriers had broken apart and the transplants had died. My opinion on the cause of failure? The barriers weren't properly anchored, and the shoots had no adequate substrate to establish roots in.

The Experimental Approach - Testing Solutions in the Field

Armed with this analysis, I designed a small-scale field experiment to test solutions for each pressure source. The approach was deliberately simple: if you want to understand what works, test the variables systematically rather than hoping for a silver bullet solution.

The restoration technique centered on fabric bags filled with nutrient-rich soil; this addressed two pressure sources directly: providing stable anchoring material and delivering the high nitrogen content Phragmites craves. The bags would be partially submerged, allowing roots to establish in controlled conditions before spreading into the natural lakebed.

For wave protection, I rebuilt the barrier system that had failed in the provincial attempt, but with better anchoring and positioning. Learning from the 2023 failure, this barrier needed to be more robust and properly secured to the lake bottom.

The experimental design tested six Phragmites australis specimens across different conditions:

  • 2 control specimen planted directly in the natural lakebed at different water depths, without protection or special substrate
  • 2 specimens in shallow water: one with soil bag only, one with soil bag plus protective netting
  • 2 specimens in deeper water: testing the same variables at different depths

This setup would reveal which factors mattered most: water depth, soil supplementation, or animal protection. Each specimen was tagged and mapped for regular monitoring.

Implementation reality hit immediately. The plants arrived damaged from shipping, and I had to improvise with materials — jute bags instead of the planned fabric, plastic nets repurposed for protection. But that's field work: you adapt with what's available and move forward.

The experiment launched in August 2025, with the first monitoring planned for September to assess initial establishment success.

Early Results and Lessons Learned

September monitoring brought encouraging news: most specimens had started sprouting new shoots, showing that the basic approach was working.

The water depth variable revealed clear patterns. The control specimen in deep water struggled, confirming that establishment needs the right moisture balance. Plants in shallow water with soil supplementation performed best, showing vigorous new growth and healthy green shoots.

What surprised me most was how well the plants recovered despite arriving in terrible condition from shipping — many looked half-dead but still managed to sprout new growth within weeks. The other surprise was discovering that excessive water depth was the real problem, not insufficient moisture as I'd initially worried about.

What didn't surprise me, but proved important nonetheless, was confirming that substrate really matters — you can't just drop plants on the lakebed and hope they'll establish roots. Both the Phragmites literature and previous restoration attempts had largely overlooked this basic requirement, but my experience with terrestrial plants made it obvious that soil conditions would be crucial. More experienced practitioners, like those behind the LIFE Lagoon Refresh project, had already demonstrated the effectiveness of using soil-filled containers for reed transplantation, which gave me confidence in the approach.

Conclusion – for now

This reed restoration project taught me that sometimes the best insights come from stepping away from the screen and getting your hands dirty. The scientific literature analysis phase was crucial: without understanding the pressure sources through the FloraLago report, I would have been shooting in the dark. But the field implementation revealed nuances that no amount of remote sensing or literature review could capture.

The systematic experimental approach, borrowed from my research science background, proved invaluable for testing multiple variables simultaneously. Instead of trying one big intervention and hoping for the best, testing six different conditions will provide clear evidence about what actually works in Lake Orta's specific conditions.

The early results are promising, but the real test comes with winter survival and long-term establishment. If even a few specimens make it through to next growing season, we'll have proven that low-cost, systematic restoration is possible. More importantly, we'll understand which factors matter most for future scaling efforts.

This experience has reinforced my conviction that effective environmental consulting requires both analytical rigor and field expertise. Whether I'm analyzing satellite imagery for agricultural projects or designing monitoring systems for climate adaptation, understanding what actually happens on the ground makes the data work more meaningful and the recommendations more practical.

For Lake Orta specifically, the next steps involve monitoring through winter and spring, then potentially scaling successful techniques to larger areas. The 50€ material cost proves this approach could work for community-led restoration efforts, not just large institutional projects.
