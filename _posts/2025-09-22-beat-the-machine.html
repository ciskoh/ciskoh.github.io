How I built a mini-game to help data science students intuitively understand machine learning's core algorithm

I created a Streamlit-based mini-game where students manually optimize a function to understand gradient descent before learning the math. After seeing students struggle with the abstract concept during my time as a student, I built this interactive tool for my upcoming teaching week at Constructor Academy. The game lets learners "be the machine" — manually finding optimal parameters — which makes the algorithmic logic click before adding complexity like learning rates and optimizers.

Introduction: Why Developing intuition Matters

Next week, I'll be teaching data science fundamentals to a fresh cohort at Constructor Academy, and gradient descent sits right at the heart of the curriculum. It's unavoidable — this algorithm powers everything from simple linear regression to the attention mechanisms that make modern AI work.

But here's the thing: I still remember being a student in that same program, staring at equations and step-by-step explanations that just wouldn't stick. The math made sense on paper, but the intuition? That took much longer to develop. Only when I could visualize the process did gradient descent finally click.

As a visual learner, I needed to see it working before I could understand why it worked. Once that foundation was solid, everything else followed: learning rates, optimizers, even more complex concepts like reinforcement learning and attention mechanisms. So with my teaching week approaching, I decided to build something that would give my students that same "aha" moment from day one.

What is Gradient Descent?

Gradient descent is the way a machine learning algorithm improves its fitting to the data by progressively changing the parameters. The algorithm calculates how much each parameter should change to reduce the prediction error, then updates all parameters simultaneously. This process repeats until the model converges to optimal (or near-optimal) parameter values. The mathematics involves computing partial derivatives of a loss function with respect to each parameter, then moving in the direction that minimizes error.

Gradient descent is the most important and general component of any machine learning algorithm. Whether you're training a simple linear regression or a complex neural network, you're using gradient descent to optimize parameters. It's unavoidable.

The challenge? For people not used to mathematical thinking, gradient descent demands a serious cognitive leap. You need to think in terms of partial derivatives, navigate multi-dimensional parameter landscapes, and understand concepts like local minima. This mathematical abstraction is a stretch for many beginners.

Solution: Let Students Be The Machine

My approach: let students manually optimize parameters before showing them the algorithm. The game presents a simple linear regression problem — random data points on the left, and students provide values for intercept and slope to fit a line.

The magic happens on the right side of the screen. Every attempt gets plotted as a point showing epochs (tries) on the x-axis and RMSE (error) on the y-axis. Students watch their own loss curve evolve as they search for better parameters.

What they discover is remarkable. Even with just two parameters, the landscape is surprisingly complex. Students quickly realize that random guessing is inefficient. They start developing strategies — adjusting one parameter at a time, making smaller changes when getting closer, recognizing when they've hit a local minimum.

This manual exploration reveals why gradient descent is so clever. Students experience firsthand the challenge of parameter optimization, then see how an algorithm can solve it systematically. The loss curve they've been building manually suddenly makes perfect sense when connected to the algorithmic approach.

Requirements-Driven Development

I started with educational requirements, not code: students need to experience manual parameter optimization before learning the algorithm. They have to explore the parameter space and reason in terms of loss optimization.

I wanted them to see both the geometric problem (fitting a line) and the optimization problem (minimizing error). Hence the dual-plot interface: data points and line fit on the left, loss tracking on the right.

Streamlit proved perfect for this kind of rapid educational prototyping. Clear requirements plus the right tool meant going from idea to working game in just a few hours.

Conclusions

IMHO The game achieves its core goal: students understand optimization challenges before learning the algorithm. Once they've manually struggled with parameter landscapes, concepts like learning rates, optimizers, and local minima become natural extensions rather than abstract hurdles.

My key insight as an educator: intuition first, mathematics second. Students who grasp why gradient descent is needed learn the how much faster. To be clear this might not be the best method for all people. Those who have a more procedural way of thinking will be able to follow the equations later on
